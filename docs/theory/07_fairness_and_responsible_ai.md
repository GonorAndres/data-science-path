# Fairness and Responsible AI

## Why this matters

ML systems make decisions that affect people. Credit approvals, hiring, medical triage, pricing, content moderation. Even a decision as mundane as what product to recommend can perpetuate structural inequality when the training data reflects past discrimination or unequal access.

Mexican context adds specifics:

- **CONAPRED and ley federal para prevenir y eliminar la discriminacion** prohibit discrimination on multiple protected grounds.
- **CNBV** (banking regulator) is beginning to require model audit trails for credit decisions.
- **EU AI Act** applies to any product sold into the EU, reaching Mexican firms with international customers.
- **Public scrutiny.** High-profile cases (COMPAS recidivism, Apple Card credit lines, etc.) illustrate reputational risk.

## Definitions of fairness

There is no single definition. The field has formalized several, and they are mutually incompatible in general (Chouldechova 2017, Kleinberg 2016).

### Demographic parity (statistical parity)

The probability of a positive prediction is equal across groups:

$$
P(\hat{Y} = 1 | A = a) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 | A = b)
$$

for all pairs $(a, b)$ of protected attribute values.

Useful when the goal is equal outcomes. Can force unqualified candidates through to equalize rates.

### Equal opportunity

True positive rates are equal across groups:

$$
P(\hat{Y} = 1 | Y = 1, A = a) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 | Y = 1, A = b)
$$

Among those who should get a positive outcome, the model approves the same share across groups.

### Equalized odds

Both true positive and false positive rates are equal across groups. Stronger than equal opportunity.

### Predictive parity

Precision is equal across groups:

$$
P(Y = 1 | \hat{Y} = 1, A = a) = P(Y = 1 | \hat{Y} = 1, A = b)
$$

Among those predicted positive, the actual positive rate is the same across groups.

### Individual fairness

Similar individuals (by some task-appropriate metric) receive similar predictions. Operationally hard to define; rarely enforced directly.

### Counterfactual fairness

The prediction would be the same if the protected attribute were counterfactually different. Requires a causal model.

## The impossibility result

Chouldechova and Kleinberg independently showed that when base rates differ across groups, you cannot satisfy predictive parity and equalized odds simultaneously. Real-world data almost always has different base rates. The field has to choose which definition matters for which application, then state the tradeoff.

## How bias enters

1. **Historical data.** Past decisions were biased; the model reproduces the bias.
2. **Sampling bias.** Some groups are underrepresented in training, leading to worse performance for those groups.
3. **Feature proxies.** Zip code proxies for race; name proxies for gender; school attended proxies for socioeconomic background.
4. **Label bias.** The label itself was generated by a biased process (arrests vs crimes; audits vs violations; promotions vs capability).
5. **Feedback loops.** Biased predictions influence future data; bias compounds.

## Fairness audit workflow

### 1. Define the protected attributes

Gender, age group, ethnicity, disability, socioeconomic class. In Mexico, include `indigena / no indigena` as relevant where data exists.

### 2. Segment performance metrics

For each protected attribute, compute the model's metrics per subgroup:

- Accuracy, precision, recall by group.
- False positive rate and false negative rate.
- Calibration (predicted probability vs observed outcome) per group.
- Disparate impact ratio ($P(\hat{Y}=1|A=a) / P(\hat{Y}=1|A=b)$).

### 3. Visualize

Reliability diagrams per group. Confusion matrices side by side. ROC / PR curves per group.

### 4. Investigate

If a disparity exists:

- Is the base rate different across groups (sometimes legitimate, e.g., age-related medical risk)?
- Are features proxy for the protected attribute? Drop, transform, or add constraints.
- Is the sample size for the minority group too small? Collect more data or re-weight.
- Are labels themselves biased? This is the hardest to fix.

### 5. Mitigate

Options, from simpler to more invasive:

- **Pre-processing.** Reweight training examples; transform features to remove proxies; generate synthetic balanced data.
- **In-processing.** Add fairness constraints to the training objective (e.g., adversarial debiasing; regularization against disparate impact).
- **Post-processing.** Threshold adjustment per group to equalize error rates; recalibrate probabilities per group.

### 6. Re-evaluate

Measure fairness metrics after mitigation. Check for regressions on overall accuracy. Document the tradeoff: you usually lose some aggregate accuracy to buy fairness.

## Model cards

Publish a model card (Mitchell et al. 2019) with:

- Model details (architecture, training data, training date).
- Intended use cases.
- Out-of-scope uses.
- Performance overall and disaggregated by subgroup.
- Known limitations and failure modes.
- Fairness analysis results.
- Ethical considerations.
- Contact for questions.

Model cards are now common practice at large AI labs and at many regulated Mexican firms.

## Datasheets for datasets

Gebru et al. 2018 proposed documentation for the dataset itself: collection process, consent, demographics, limitations. Essential when your model's data story is complex.

## Responsible AI beyond fairness

Fairness is one dimension. Responsible AI also includes:

- **Privacy.** Differential privacy, federated learning, data minimization.
- **Security.** Adversarial robustness, model stealing, prompt injection (for LLMs).
- **Accountability.** Audit trails, model versioning, rollback capability.
- **Transparency.** Clear documentation of decisions and their rationale.
- **Human oversight.** Humans in the loop for high-stakes decisions.
- **Environmental impact.** Compute cost, carbon footprint of large-model training.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming "we removed the protected attribute" means the model is fair. Proxies do the same work.
- Using a single fairness metric without stating why. The interviewer will push back.
- Fixing training-time bias but deploying on live data where the bias returns.
- Treating fairness as a checkbox at the end. Fairness work starts at problem framing.
- Ignoring intersectional analysis. A model fair to men and fair to women may still be unfair to women-of-color.

## The 80% rule worked example

The US EEOC's "four-fifths rule" (29 CFR Part 1607) declares that a selection rate for any protected group below 80 percent of the rate for the most-selected group is **prima facie evidence of adverse impact**. The number is a regulatory threshold, not a statistical one, but it is a widely adopted starting point and has been imported into Mexican corporate compliance practice.

Concrete example: an automated hiring screen passes 60 percent of male applicants and 42 percent of female applicants. The adverse impact ratio is

$$
\text{AIR} = \frac{P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = \text{female})}{P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = \text{male})} = \frac{0.42}{0.60} = 0.70
$$

$0.70 < 0.80$, so the screen fails the four-fifths rule and triggers further review. The same calculation applies to insurance underwriting decisions, credit approvals, and any binary classifier whose output is consequential.

Caveats. The rule does not establish that discrimination occurred; it shifts the burden of proof to the employer or insurer to show business necessity and the absence of a less-discriminatory alternative. A passing AIR (above 0.80) does not absolve a model: if the disparity is statistically significant on a large sample, courts and regulators may still act.

## Intersectional fairness

Evaluating each protected attribute independently misses harms concentrated at intersections. A model can satisfy the 80% rule on `gender` and on `ethnicity` separately while producing severely worse outcomes for indigenous women than for any single-attribute subgroup. Crenshaw (1989) named this; Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) demonstrated it empirically in commercial face-recognition systems where error rates for dark-skinned women exceeded those for light-skinned men by an order of magnitude even though aggregate error and per-attribute error were within tolerance.

**Sample-size requirement.** To detect a true subgroup disparity of size $\delta$ (e.g., a 10 percentage-point gap in true-positive rate) with power $1 - \beta = 0.80$ at significance $\alpha = 0.05$, the per-subgroup sample size needed is approximately

$$
n_{\text{subgroup}} \approx \frac{2 (z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2 p(1-p)}{\delta^2}
$$

For $p = 0.5$ and $\delta = 0.10$ this is roughly 200 per subgroup. With four protected attributes of two levels each, the smallest cell needs 200 examples; if any cell has fewer, the audit cannot detect harms at that resolution. This is why intersectional fairness audits are often impossible on small datasets and why deliberate over-sampling of intersectional cells is part of responsible data collection.

## Proxy detection via adversarial classifier

Dropping the protected attribute does not remove proxies. The high-signal test for proxy leakage:

1. Hold out the protected attribute $A$ as a label.
2. Train a classifier that predicts $A$ from the remaining feature set $X$.
3. Evaluate on a held-out split. If AUC is significantly above 0.5 (use a permutation test or compute a binomial CI), the feature set encodes $A$ and the downstream model is at risk of indirect discrimination.

Implementation note. The classifier should be at least as expressive as the target model; a linear logistic regression often misses non-linear proxies that a gradient-boosted tree picks up. Inspect the importance of features in the adversarial classifier: those features are the proxies. Common offenders in Mexican credit data: zip code, employer NAICS code, primary banking institution, average ATM withdrawal location.

## Differential privacy

A randomized algorithm $M$ is **$(\epsilon, \delta)$-differentially private** if for every pair of datasets $D, D'$ that differ in one record and every measurable output set $S$,

$$
P(M(D) \in S) \leq e^\epsilon \, P(M(D') \in S) + \delta
$$

Smaller $\epsilon$ means stronger privacy. $\delta$ is a small slack term, typically set to $1/n$ or smaller; it allows a tiny probability of catastrophic privacy loss in exchange for tractability.

The privacy-utility tradeoff is unavoidable. Tighter $\epsilon$ requires more noise injection, which lowers utility (model accuracy, query precision). For statistics released over millions of records the standard practice is $\epsilon \in [0.1, 1]$ for sensitive data and $\epsilon \in [1, 10]$ for less sensitive applications.

Two canonical noise mechanisms for releasing a numeric statistic $f(D)$:

- **Laplace mechanism.** Add noise drawn from $\text{Laplace}(0, \Delta f / \epsilon)$ where $\Delta f$ is the $L_1$ sensitivity (max change in $f$ from changing one record). Achieves pure $\epsilon$-DP with $\delta = 0$.
- **Gaussian mechanism.** Add $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ noise with $\sigma = \Delta_2 f \sqrt{2 \ln(1.25/\delta)} / \epsilon$. Achieves $(\epsilon, \delta)$-DP. Composes more cleanly under repeated queries.

Numeric example. To release the mean claim amount over a portfolio of 10,000 policies where any one claim can shift the mean by at most 1 USD (i.e., $\Delta f = 1/10000$), at $\epsilon = 1$ the Laplace noise scale is $0.0001$, which is invisible. The privacy budget binds when many such queries are released or when sensitivity is high (per-individual outliers).

## EU AI Act risk tier breakdown

The EU AI Act (in force from 2024, full applicability phasing through 2026) classifies AI systems into four risk tiers. Each tier has distinct obligations and each has insurance-relevant examples.

| Tier | Examples in insurance | Obligations |
|------|----------------------|-------------|
| Unacceptable | Social-scoring of policyholders by government; emotion recognition in claim disputes; subliminal manipulation in renewal upsell | Prohibited outright |
| High | Health insurance underwriting; life insurance pricing affecting access; fraud-detection models that can deny claims; credit-scoring linked to insurance offers | Risk management system, data governance, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, post-market monitoring, conformity assessment |
| Limited | Chatbots interacting with policyholders; AI-generated marketing copy | Transparency: user must be told they are interacting with AI |
| Minimal | Spam filters in claims email triage; spell-checkers in agent UIs | No specific obligations beyond general safety |

A Mexican insurer selling to EU residents falls under the Act regardless of where the model is trained or hosted. The high-risk obligations are operationally heavy and require the documentation set produced by a feature store, model registry, and monitoring pipeline as described in `docs/theory/13_mlops_architecture_patterns.md`.

## Mexican context specifics

- Name-based ethnicity inference is a live debate. CONAPRED and INEGI have published guidelines.
- `CURP` encodes gender (positions 11) and birth date (positions 5 to 10). The gender character is `H` or `M`. Including raw CURP in feature pipelines effectively reintroduces gender as a feature even after explicit gender drop. Workaround: hash the CURP after deleting positions 5 to 11, or replace CURP with a non-reversible internal customer ID at ingestion. Document the transformation in the data contract.
- Credit bureau scores (Buro de Credito, Circulo de Credito) have baseline biases linked to formal-economy participation.
- Informal economy workers are underrepresented in many datasets; downstream models underperform on their demographics.

### CONAPRED-style audit checklist

Inspired by the CONAPRED 2024 study and the protected attributes enumerated in the **Ley Federal para Prevenir y Eliminar la Discriminacion** (LFPED, articulo 1 fraccion III), every model audit in a Mexican context should answer the following before deployment:

1. Does the dataset contain or proxy for any of the LFPED protected attributes: origen etnico o nacional, color de piel, cultura, sexo, genero, edad, discapacidad, condicion social o economica, condiciones de salud, religion, opiniones, preferencias sexuales, estado civil, embarazo, lengua, identidad de genero?
2. For each attribute present, is the disparate impact ratio computed and below 0.80?
3. Has the adversarial-classifier proxy detection been run on the feature set with the attribute as the label, and the AUC reported?
4. For features identified as proxies, has a business-necessity justification been documented?
5. If the model is used in credit scoring, has the **CNBV circular** on algorithmic credit decisions been reviewed (the current draft requires explainability artifacts and an audit log of denied applications, with model documentation that maps to model-card fields)?
6. Are predictions disaggregated by subgroup in the production monitoring dashboard, with alerts for drift in the per-subgroup metric?
7. Is there a documented appeals process for individuals who receive an adverse decision, including reason codes and a human reviewer?
8. Has the data subject been informed of automated decision-making in line with the **LFPDPPP** (Ley Federal de Proteccion de Datos Personales en Posesion de Particulares)?

Numbered yes/no answers, signed by both the model owner and a compliance reviewer, satisfy the working interpretation of the CNBV draft circular for algorithmic credit scoring as of 2026. Treat this list as a starting point; the regulator's interpretation is evolving and the list should be reviewed every six months.
