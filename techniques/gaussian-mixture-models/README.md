# Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)

## What it is

A Gaussian Mixture Model is a probabilistic model that assumes your data was generated by a mixture of several Gaussian (normal) distributions, each with its own mean and covariance. Unlike K-means, which makes a hard assignment ("this point belongs to cluster 3"), GMMs make **soft assignments** -- they tell you the probability that each data point belongs to each cluster. This makes GMMs a flexible, principled approach to clustering that can capture elliptical and overlapping cluster shapes, not just the spherical blobs that K-means favors.

## Why it matters

In actuarial and insurance work, risk rarely falls into clean, mutually exclusive buckets. A policyholder might exhibit characteristics of both a "low-risk commuter" and a "moderate-risk urban driver" segments. Hard clustering forces a binary choice; soft clustering via GMMs acknowledges the ambiguity and gives you a richer, more honest picture.

Specific benefits for actuarial/data science practitioners:

- **Risk segmentation**: Segment policyholders into risk tiers where the boundaries are fuzzy, not crisp. A policyholder with 60% probability of belonging to the "high-risk" segment and 40% to "moderate-risk" carries more information than a binary label.
- **Anomaly detection**: Points with low probability under all mixture components are natural outliers -- useful for fraud detection or identifying unusual claims patterns.
- **Density estimation**: GMMs give you a full probability density function over your data, which is useful for simulation, pricing, and generating synthetic data.
- **Foundation for more complex models**: Understanding GMMs and the EM algorithm is a gateway to hidden Markov models, Bayesian networks, and other latent variable models used in advanced actuarial modeling.

## When to use it

- You want to cluster data but suspect that clusters overlap or have different shapes and sizes.
- You need a **probability of cluster membership** rather than a hard label (e.g., "this policyholder is 73% likely to be in the high-risk segment").
- Your clusters are elliptical rather than spherical. K-means assumes spherical clusters of equal size; GMMs do not.
- You want a generative model that can produce new synthetic data points by sampling from the fitted distribution.
- You need anomaly detection via density estimation -- data points in low-density regions of the mixture are anomalies.
- You want to model the distribution of a continuous variable (like claim severity) as a mixture of sub-populations (e.g., small nuisance claims vs. large catastrophic claims).
- You are building a risk segmentation framework where soft boundaries are more appropriate than hard cutoffs.

## How it works (intuitive)

### The mental model

Imagine you are looking down at a map of a city at night. You see several clusters of streetlights. Some clusters are dense and compact (a downtown area), others are spread out and elongated (a highway strip). A few lights sit between clusters, and it is not obvious which cluster they belong to.

**K-means** would draw hard circular boundaries and assign every light to exactly one cluster. **GMM** would say: "I see K clusters, each one is a cloud of probability (a Gaussian bell curve in 2D). For any given streetlight, I can tell you the probability it was produced by each cloud."

### Step-by-step process

1. **Assume K Gaussian distributions exist** in your data. Each Gaussian is defined by:
   - A **mean** (center of the cluster)
   - A **covariance matrix** (shape and orientation of the cluster -- round, elongated, tilted)
   - A **mixing weight** (how much of the overall data this cluster explains)

2. **Initialize** the parameters. Common strategies:
   - Use K-means results as a starting point (recommended)
   - Random initialization (can get stuck in bad local optima)
   - K-means++ style initialization

3. **Run the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm**:

   **E-step (Expectation)**: For each data point, compute the probability (responsibility) that each of the K Gaussians generated it. A point near the center of Gaussian 2 gets high responsibility for Gaussian 2 and low for the others. A point sitting right between two Gaussians gets moderate responsibility for both.

   **M-step (Maximization)**: Re-estimate each Gaussian's mean, covariance, and mixing weight using the responsibilities as soft weights. Points with high responsibility for Gaussian 2 pull its mean and shape more strongly.

4. **Repeat E-step and M-step** until the parameters converge (stop changing meaningfully) or you hit a maximum iteration count.

5. **Result**: You have K Gaussians, each with learned mean, covariance, and weight. For any data point (new or old), you can compute the probability it belongs to each Gaussian.

### The EM algorithm analogy

Think of fitting a GMM like positioning K adjustable spotlights to best illuminate a crowd of people in a dark room:

- **E-step**: Given the current spotlight positions and shapes, figure out how much each person is lit by each spotlight (their "responsibility" to each light).
- **M-step**: Given how much each spotlight is responsible for illuminating each person, adjust each spotlight's position (mean), beam width/shape (covariance), and brightness (mixing weight) to best cover the people it is responsible for.
- Repeat until the spotlights stop moving.

### GMM vs. K-means comparison

| Feature | K-means | GMM |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Assignment | Hard (each point in exactly one cluster) | Soft (probability of membership in each cluster) |
| Cluster shape | Spherical only | Elliptical (any covariance structure) |
| Cluster size | Assumes roughly equal | Can have different sizes |
| Output | Cluster labels | Cluster probabilities + full density model |
| Speed | Very fast | Slower (but still practical for moderate data) |
| Sensitivity to init | High | High (use K-means init to help) |
| Theoretical basis | Distance minimization | Maximum likelihood via EM |

## Key concepts

### Mathematical formulation

A GMM models the probability density of data $\mathbf{x}$ as:

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)$$

Where:
- $K$ is the number of mixture components (clusters)
- $\pi_k$ is the mixing weight of component $k$ (with $\sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k = 1$ and $\pi_k \geq 0$)
- $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)$ is the multivariate Gaussian density with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$

The multivariate Gaussian density is:

$$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right)$$

### EM algorithm details

**E-step**: Compute the responsibility of component $k$ for data point $\mathbf{x}_i$:

$$\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)}$$

This is just Bayes' theorem: the posterior probability that point $i$ came from component $k$.

**M-step**: Update parameters using the responsibilities:

$$N_k = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma_{ik}$$
$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma_{ik} \, \mathbf{x}_i$$
$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma_{ik} (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\text{new}})(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{\text{new}})^T$$
$$\pi_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{N_k}{N}$$

### Covariance type

The shape of the covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$ controls cluster shape. scikit-learn offers four options:

| Type | Description | Degrees of freedom | Use when |
|------|-------------|-------------------|----------|
| `full` | Each component has its own general covariance matrix | Highest | Clusters have different shapes and orientations |
| `tied` | All components share the same covariance matrix | Moderate | Clusters have the same shape but different locations |
| `diag` | Each component has its own diagonal covariance (axis-aligned ellipses) | Moderate | Features are independent within clusters |
| `spherical` | Each component has a single variance (spherical clusters) | Lowest | Essentially K-means with soft assignments |

### Model selection: BIC and AIC

How do you choose K? Use information criteria:

**Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)**:
$$\text{BIC} = -2 \ln(\hat{L}) + p \ln(N)$$

**Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)**:
$$\text{AIC} = -2 \ln(\hat{L}) + 2p$$

Where:
- $\hat{L}$ is the maximized likelihood
- $p$ is the number of free parameters
- $N$ is the number of data points

**Lower BIC/AIC is better.** BIC penalizes model complexity more heavily than AIC, so it tends to select simpler models. In practice:

1. Fit GMMs for K = 1, 2, 3, ..., K_max.
2. Compute BIC (or AIC) for each.
3. Pick the K with the lowest BIC.

BIC is generally preferred for clustering because it is more conservative and less prone to overfitting.

## Python implementation

### Basic GMM with scikit-learn

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Fit a GMM with 3 components
gmm = GaussianMixture(
    n_components=3,
    covariance_type='full',
    n_init=10,           # Run 10 times with different initializations
    random_state=42
)
gmm.fit(X)

# Soft assignments (probabilities)
probabilities = gmm.predict_proba(X)  # Shape: (n_samples, n_components)

# Hard assignments (most likely cluster)
labels = gmm.predict(X)

# Log-likelihood of data
log_likelihood = gmm.score(X)  # Per-sample average log-likelihood

# Access learned parameters
print("Means:", gmm.means_)
print("Covariances:", gmm.covariances_)
print("Weights:", gmm.weights_)
```

### Model selection with BIC

```python
bic_scores = []
aic_scores = []
K_range = range(1, 11)

for k in K_range:
    gmm = GaussianMixture(n_components=k, covariance_type='full',
                           n_init=5, random_state=42)
    gmm.fit(X)
    bic_scores.append(gmm.bic(X))
    aic_scores.append(gmm.aic(X))

optimal_k = K_range[np.argmin(bic_scores)]
print(f"Optimal K by BIC: {optimal_k}")

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.plot(K_range, bic_scores, 'o-', label='BIC')
plt.plot(K_range, aic_scores, 's--', label='AIC')
plt.xlabel('Number of Components (K)')
plt.ylabel('Information Criterion')
plt.title('GMM Model Selection')
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

### Anomaly detection with GMMs

```python
# Fit GMM on training data
gmm = GaussianMixture(n_components=5, covariance_type='full', random_state=42)
gmm.fit(X_train)

# Score new data -- low scores indicate anomalies
log_probs = gmm.score_samples(X_test)
threshold = np.percentile(log_probs, 5)  # Bottom 5% as anomalies

```

## Gotchas & lessons learned

### Singularity issues
If a Gaussian component collapses onto a single data point or a very small subset, its covariance matrix can become singular (non-invertible), and the likelihood shoots to infinity. Regularization helps: scikit-learn's `reg_covar` parameter adds a small value to the diagonal of the covariance matrix to prevent this.

### Initialization matters a lot
EM is guaranteed to increase the likelihood at each step, but it converges to a local maximum, not necessarily the global one. Always run multiple initializations (`n_init=10` or more) and keep the best result.

### High-dimensional data
GMMs struggle in high dimensions because the number of parameters in the covariance matrix grows quadratically with the number of features. Use dimensionality reduction (PCA) before fitting a GMM if you have more than ~20 features, or use `covariance_type='diag'` to reduce parameters.

### Choosing covariance type
Start with `'full'` for maximum flexibility. If you get singularity warnings or have limited data relative to dimensions, step down to `'diag'` or `'tied'`. The BIC comparison across covariance types can also guide this choice.

### GMMs assume Gaussian components
If your data has fundamentally non-Gaussian structure (e.g., heavy tails, discrete spikes), GMMs may need many components to approximate the true density, leading to spurious clusters. Consider kernel density estimation or other methods for highly non-Gaussian data.

### Soft assignments are the real value
The main advantage of GMMs over K-means is the probabilistic output. If you are just going to call `predict()` and use hard labels, you are throwing away the richest part of the model. Use `predict_proba()` and incorporate the uncertainty into downstream analyses.

### Scaling your features
Like K-means, GMMs are sensitive to feature scales. Standardize or normalize your features before fitting. A feature measured in dollars (range: 0-1,000,000) will dominate one measured in percentages (range: 0-1) if you do not scale.

### Number of components is not always the number of "real" clusters
Sometimes the best-fitting GMM uses more components than there are "real" clusters, because it needs multiple Gaussians to approximate a single non-Gaussian cluster. Interpret BIC-optimal K with domain knowledge.
