# Cohort Analysis

## What it is

Cohort analysis is a technique for tracking groups of customers (cohorts) who share a common starting event -- like the month they first purchased, the quarter they signed up, or the campaign that acquired them -- and observing how their behavior changes over time. Instead of looking at aggregate metrics that blend together customers of different ages, cohort analysis separates them so you can see whether your January sign-ups behave differently from your June sign-ups. This separation is what makes it possible to distinguish genuine trends (like improving retention) from composition effects (like having more new customers diluting your averages).

## Why it matters

- **Retention is the heartbeat of a business.** Acquiring customers is expensive; keeping them is profitable. Cohort analysis is the primary tool for measuring whether you are actually retaining customers or just replacing churned ones with new ones.
- **Insurance-specific value**: In insurance, policy cohorts are natural -- everyone who bought a policy in Q1 2024 forms a cohort. Tracking their renewal rates, claim frequencies, and premium growth over policy years is standard actuarial practice. Cohort analysis formalizes this into a repeatable analytical framework.
- **Detects hidden problems**: Aggregate metrics can mask deterioration. If your overall revenue is growing 10% but your 2024 cohorts retain at 60% versus 75% for 2023 cohorts, you have a problem that aggregate numbers hide.
- **Evaluates interventions**: Did the new onboarding flow improve retention? Compare the retention curve of the pre-change cohort to the post-change cohort. This is more rigorous than before/after comparisons on aggregate metrics.
- **Connects to survival analysis**: Cohort retention curves are discrete-time survival functions. If you are comfortable with survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier, Cox PH), cohort analysis is the business-friendly version of the same idea.

## When to use it

- You want to measure customer retention or churn over time, broken out by when customers were acquired.
- You are evaluating whether a product change, pricing change, or marketing campaign affected customer behavior.
- You need to forecast future revenue by modeling how each acquisition cohort will behave as it ages.
- You are building a customer lifetime value model and need empirical retention curves as inputs.
- You want to compare the "quality" of customers acquired through different channels (organic vs. paid vs. referral).
- You are doing actuarial experience studies on policy cohorts (e.g., lapse rates by issue year).
- You have a subscription or recurring-revenue business and want to understand your unit economics over time.

## How it works (intuitive)

### The classroom analogy

Imagine a school that admits a new class every September. If you want to know whether the school is getting better at teaching, you would not just look at the average test score across all students -- that mixes freshmen with seniors. Instead, you would track each entering class separately: "The Class of 2020 scored X after one year, Y after two years. The Class of 2021 scored A after one year, B after two years." Now you can compare apples to apples.

Cohort analysis does exactly this for customers.

### Step-by-step process

**Step 1: Define the cohort**

Choose what event defines cohort membership:
- **Acquisition cohort** (most common): The month or quarter the customer first transacted, signed up, or was acquired.
- **Behavioral cohort**: Customers who performed a specific action (e.g., "customers who used feature X in their first 30 days").
- **Segment cohort**: Customers grouped by a characteristic at the time of acquisition (e.g., channel, geography, product tier).

**Step 2: Define the time periods**

Choose the granularity for tracking:
- Monthly periods are most common for consumer businesses.
- Quarterly or annual periods work better for insurance, B2B, or low-frequency businesses.
- Label periods as "Period 0" (the cohort month itself), "Period 1" (one period later), etc.

**Step 3: Define the metric**

What are you tracking over time?
- **Retention rate**: Percentage of the cohort still active in each subsequent period.
- **Revenue per cohort member**: Average spend per original cohort member (including those who churned).
- **Cumulative revenue**: Total revenue generated by the cohort through each period.
- **Claim frequency**: For insurance, the rate of claims per exposure unit per period.

**Step 4: Build the cohort matrix**

This is the core output -- a triangular matrix where:
- Rows = cohorts (e.g., Jan 2024, Feb 2024, ...)
- Columns = periods since cohort start (Period 0, Period 1, ...)
- Cell values = the metric (e.g., retention rate)

It is triangular because newer cohorts have not yet reached later periods.

| Cohort | Period 0 | Period 1 | Period 2 | Period 3 |
|--------|----------|----------|----------|----------|
| Jan 2024 | 100% | 72% | 58% | 51% |
| Feb 2024 | 100% | 75% | 61% | -- |
| Mar 2024 | 100% | 70% | -- | -- |
| Apr 2024 | 100% | -- | -- | -- |

**Step 5: Visualize and interpret**

- **Heatmap**: Color-code the matrix so that patterns jump out. Darker = higher retention.
- **Retention curves**: Plot each cohort as a line, with period on the x-axis and retention on the y-axis. Compare how the curves stack up.
- **Churn waterfall**: Show how many customers drop off in each period, broken out by cohort.

**Step 6: Compare and act**

- Compare cohort curves to identify trends: Are newer cohorts retaining better or worse?
- Identify the "critical period": Most churn typically happens in the first 1-2 periods. If you can improve early retention, the compounding effect is large.
- Test hypotheses: If you changed your onboarding in March, the March cohort should show a different retention curve than February.

### The leaky bucket analogy

Think of each cohort as a bucket of water. When you acquire the cohort, the bucket is full. Over time, water leaks out (customers churn). The retention curve shows how fast each bucket leaks. Cohort analysis lets you compare buckets: "The January bucket leaked 30% in the first month, but the March bucket only leaked 20% -- what changed?" Your goal is to make the buckets leak more slowly.

## Key concepts

### Retention rate

$$\text{Retention}_{c,t} = \frac{|\text{Active customers in cohort } c \text{ at period } t|}{|\text{Total customers in cohort } c \text{ at period } 0|}$$

This is the proportion of the original cohort that is still active at period $t$. Note that the denominator is always the *original* cohort size, not the previous period's count. This gives you the cumulative retention rate.

### Period-over-period retention (conditional retention)

$$\text{Conditional Retention}_{c,t} = \frac{|\text{Active at period } t|}{|\text{Active at period } t-1|}$$

This tells you what fraction of customers who survived to period $t-1$ also survived to period $t$. It is the discrete-time analog of the hazard rate complement in survival analysis.

$$\text{Cumulative Retention}_{c,t} = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \text{Conditional Retention}_{c,s}$$

### Churn rate

$$\text{Churn}_{c,t} = 1 - \text{Conditional Retention}_{c,t}$$

Or equivalently:

$$\text{Cumulative Churn}_{c,t} = 1 - \text{Retention}_{c,t}$$

### Revenue cohort analysis

Instead of counting active customers, sum revenue:

$$\text{Revenue per Original Member}_{c,t} = \frac{\text{Total revenue from cohort } c \text{ in period } t}{|\text{Cohort } c \text{ at period } 0|}$$

This metric accounts for both churn and changes in spend per active customer.

### Connection to survival analysis

The cohort retention curve is a discrete-time survival function:

$$S(t) = P(T > t)$$

where $T$ is the "lifetime" of a customer. The Kaplan-Meier estimator from survival analysis is essentially a more sophisticated version of the cohort retention calculation that handles censoring (customers who have not yet had a chance to churn because they are too new).

### Types of cohorts

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Acquisition cohort | Grouped by when they first became a customer | "January 2024 sign-ups" |
| Behavioral cohort | Grouped by an action taken | "Customers who filed a claim in their first 90 days" |
| Channel cohort | Grouped by acquisition source | "Customers acquired via paid search" |
| Product cohort | Grouped by initial product | "Customers who started with auto-only vs. bundled" |
| Geographic cohort | Grouped by location | "Customers in the Southeast region" |

### Cohort analysis vs. simple time series

| Simple time series | Cohort analysis |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| "Our retention rate this quarter was 78%" | "The Q1 2024 cohort retained at 78% after one quarter; the Q2 2024 cohort retained at 82%" |
| Mixes customers of different ages | Separates customers by age |
| Cannot distinguish improving retention from growing acquisition | Clearly shows whether each cohort is retaining better or worse |
| Simpson's paradox risk | Avoids Simpson's paradox by stratifying |

## Python implementation

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def build_cohort_matrix(df, customer_col='customer_id', date_col='transaction_date',
                         freq='M'):
    """
    Build a retention cohort matrix from transaction data.

    Parameters
    ----------
    df : pd.DataFrame
        Transaction-level data.
    customer_col : str
        Customer identifier column.
    date_col : str
        Transaction date column.
    freq : str
        Period frequency ('M' for monthly, 'Q' for quarterly).

    Returns
    -------
    pd.DataFrame
        Cohort matrix with retention rates.
    """
    df = df.copy()
    df[date_col] = pd.to_datetime(df[date_col])

    # Assign cohort (first transaction period)
    df['cohort'] = df.groupby(customer_col)[date_col].transform('min').dt.to_period(freq)

    # Assign transaction period
    df['period'] = df[date_col].dt.to_period(freq)

    # Compute period index (number of periods since cohort start)
    df['period_index'] = (df['period'] - df['cohort']).apply(lambda x: x.n)

    # Count unique customers per cohort per period
    cohort_data = df.groupby(['cohort', 'period_index'])[customer_col].nunique().reset_index()
    cohort_data.columns = ['cohort', 'period_index', 'customers']

    # Pivot to matrix form
    cohort_matrix = cohort_data.pivot(index='cohort', columns='period_index', values='customers')

    # Compute retention rates (divide by period 0)
    cohort_sizes = cohort_matrix[0]
    retention_matrix = cohort_matrix.divide(cohort_sizes, axis=0)

    return retention_matrix, cohort_sizes


def plot_cohort_heatmap(retention_matrix, title='Cohort Retention Heatmap'):
    """Plot a heatmap of the retention matrix."""
    plt.figure(figsize=(12, 8))
    sns.heatmap(
        retention_matrix,
        annot=True,
        fmt='.0%',
        cmap='YlOrRd_r',
        vmin=0,
        vmax=1,
        linewidths=0.5
    )
    plt.title(title)
    plt.xlabel('Periods Since Acquisition')
    plt.ylabel('Acquisition Cohort')
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.show()
```

Key libraries:
- **pandas** for grouping, pivoting, and period arithmetic.
- **seaborn** for heatmaps.
- **plotly** for interactive retention curve plots.
- **lifelines** for survival-based cohort analysis with Kaplan-Meier curves.

## R implementation (if applicable)

```r
library(dplyr)
library(tidyr)
library(ggplot2)
library(lubridate)

build_cohort_matrix <- function(df, customer_col, date_col, freq = "month") {
  df <- df %>%
    mutate(
      txn_date = as.Date(.data[[date_col]]),
      txn_period = floor_date(txn_date, unit = freq)
    ) %>%
    group_by(.data[[customer_col]]) %>%
    mutate(cohort = min(txn_period)) %>%
    ungroup() %>%
    mutate(
      period_index = interval(cohort, txn_period) %/% months(1)
    )

  cohort_counts <- df %>%
    group_by(cohort, period_index) %>%
    summarise(customers = n_distinct(.data[[customer_col]]), .groups = "drop")

  cohort_sizes <- cohort_counts %>% filter(period_index == 0) %>% select(cohort, cohort_size = customers)

  retention <- cohort_counts %>%
    left_join(cohort_sizes, by = "cohort") %>%
    mutate(retention_rate = customers / cohort_size)

  return(retention)
}
```

**Python vs. R tradeoff**: Both languages handle cohort analysis well. Python's `pd.to_period` and pivot operations are concise. R's `lubridate` + `dplyr` pipeline is equally clean. Use whichever integrates better with your downstream analysis.

## Gotchas & lessons learned

### 1. Define "active" carefully
What counts as being "active" in a period? For a subscription, it is straightforward (they are paying). For a non-contractual business, you must define a rule: "made at least one transaction in the period" or "logged in at least once." Different definitions produce different retention curves. Be explicit and consistent.

### 2. Beware of seasonality
If you acquire more customers in December (holiday shoppers), the December cohort may show artificially low retention in January simply because many of those customers were one-time gift buyers. This is not a product problem; it is a cohort composition problem. Consider segmenting by acquisition channel or product to account for this.

### 3. Small cohorts are noisy
If a monthly cohort has only 50 customers, the retention rates will bounce around due to random variation. Consider using wider time periods (quarterly instead of monthly) or combining small cohorts. Confidence intervals on retention rates help convey uncertainty.

### 4. Survivorship bias in behavioral cohorts
If you define a behavioral cohort as "customers who used feature X in month 1," you are conditioning on survival to month 1 with enough engagement to use the feature. This cohort will mechanically show higher retention than average. Be careful about drawing causal conclusions.

### 5. The triangular gap
Newer cohorts have less data (fewer periods observed). Do not extrapolate the missing triangle naively. If you need to fill it in for forecasting, use a model (e.g., fit a retention curve to the observed data and project forward).

### 6. Cumulative vs. conditional retention
Reporting cumulative retention (% of original cohort still active) is more intuitive for stakeholders. But conditional retention (% of previous period's customers still active) is more useful for modeling because it reveals whether the churn rate is stabilizing, increasing, or decreasing over time.

### 7. Watch out for reactivations
In some businesses, customers churn and then come back. If your retention calculation counts reactivated customers as "active," the retention curve can actually increase, which looks strange. Decide upfront whether to count reactivations and document the choice.

### 8. Incomplete periods
If you are computing monthly cohort analysis on February 20, the current month is incomplete. Either exclude the current month or clearly flag it as partial data. Partial periods will always look like low retention.

### 9. Revenue cohort analysis reveals more
Retention alone can be misleading. A cohort might retain at 80%, but if the remaining customers are spending less each period, the economic value is declining. Revenue-per-original-member captures both retention and spend changes in one metric.

### 10. Cohort analysis is descriptive, not causal
Seeing that the March cohort retains better than February does not prove that a change you made in March caused the improvement. Other factors (seasonality, marketing mix, external events) could explain the difference. For causal claims, pair cohort analysis with A/B testing or causal inference methods.
